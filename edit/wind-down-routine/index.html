Let’s Build Your Perfect Wind Down Routine

You do not need a two hour nighttime routine, twelve products on your nightstand, or a completely screen free life to sleep well.

What you need is a routine that helps your brain and body shift out of daytime mode.

Your body responds to timing, light, caffeine, activity, and repeated habits. A good wind down routine uses those things to make the transition into sleep easier. The CDC recommends keeping a regular sleep schedule, creating a relaxing sleep environment, avoiding caffeine later in the day, and turning off electronics before bed.

But your routine should also solve your problem. Someone whose brain will not shut off at night may need something very different from someone whose afternoon coffee is still affecting sleep.

So instead of copying someone else’s routine, let’s build yours.

Now build the part that applies to everyone

1. Pick a realistic bedtime

Start with the time you actually want to be asleep, then work backward. Most adults need at least seven hours of sleep, and keeping your sleep and wake times fairly consistent can help support your body clock.

2. Move caffeine earlier if sleep is a problem

A 2023 systematic review and meta analysis found that caffeine reduced total sleep time by about 45 minutes on average. Based on the studies included, the researchers estimated that a coffee containing around 107 mg of caffeine would need to be consumed about nine hours before bed to avoid reducing total sleep time. People respond differently, but if you struggle to fall asleep, afternoon caffeine is an easy thing to test.

3. Lower the stimulation

Bright evening light can delay the timing of your body clock, and phones can keep your brain engaged long after you meant to stop. You do not have to ban screens all evening. Pick one boundary you can actually keep.

4. If your brain will not shut off, get tomorrow out of your head

A small laboratory study found that healthy young adults who spent five minutes writing a detailed to do list before bed fell asleep faster than those who wrote about completed tasks. It was a small study, so this is not a guaranteed sleep trick, but it is simple and useful if your thoughts are mostly about tomorrow.

5. Choose one or two things that genuinely relax you

Your routine should make you less alert, not give you another list to complete. Choose what you would actually enjoy repeating.

A warm shower is one option with useful evidence behind it. A systematic review and meta analysis found that passive body heating with a warm shower or bath before bed was associated with falling asleep faster and better self reported sleep quality. The studies suggest the benefit may relate to the drop in core body temperature that happens after warming.

Your personalized routine

Use your answer to Question 1 as your main routine. Then add the relaxing activity you chose in Question 3.

If you chose A

Your brain will not shut off

  • About 60 minutes before bed: stop work, planning, and anything likely to pull you back into problem solving.
  • About 30 minutes before bed: lower the lights and put your phone away.
  • About 15 minutes before bed: write down tomorrow’s tasks so you are not trying to hold them in your head.
  • Final activity: read, stretch, listen to something calm, or do another low stimulation activity you actually enjoy.

Your goal is to give your brain a clear ending to the day instead of carrying unfinished thoughts into bed.

If you chose B

You get a second wind at night

  • Move your last caffeine earlier for one week and see whether bedtime feels different.
  • Keep evening lighting softer.
  • Avoid highly stimulating work, scrolling, or intense conversations in the final part of the night when possible.
  • Choose one quiet activity for the final 20 to 30 minutes.

Your goal is to stop feeding that late burst of alertness and make your evening feel progressively quieter.

If you chose C

Your phone keeps you awake

  • Choose a realistic phone cutoff, such as 30 minutes before bed.
  • Charge your phone somewhere you cannot reach without getting up.
  • Move one thing you already enjoy, such as skincare, reading, or a shower, into that phone free window.
  • Go to bed when you feel sleepy instead of getting into bed to continue scrolling.

Your goal is not a perfect digital detox. It is stopping your phone from quietly adding another hour to your night.

If you chose D

You are not sleepy at bedtime

  • Keep your wake time fairly consistent.
  • Get daylight during the day, especially earlier in the day.
  • Move caffeine earlier.
  • Use softer light in the evening.
  • Do not force yourself into bed far earlier than you feel sleepy.

Your goal is to strengthen the timing cues that help your body know when to feel awake and when to feel sleepy.

If you chose E

Your body feels restless

  • Finish intense activity early enough that you have time to settle afterward.
  • Take a warm shower or bath if you enjoy it.
  • Add a few minutes of gentle stretching if that feels relaxing.
  • Lower the lights and choose something physically quiet for the final part of the night.

Your goal is to create a physical transition between being active and being in bed.

If you chose F

Your schedule changes constantly

  • Choose a realistic wake time and keep it as steady as you reasonably can.
  • Use a bedtime window instead of one exact minute, such as 10:45 PM to 11:15 PM.
  • Start the same simple wind down routine before that window each night.
  • Keep the routine short enough that you can repeat it on normal weekdays.

Your goal is consistency first. Once the pattern feels normal, you can fine tune the details.

Give it one week

Do not change five things every night and then decide nothing works.

Try the same basic routine for a week. Notice whether you get sleepy earlier, spend less time awake in bed, reach for your phone less, or feel calmer when you finally lie down.

Keep what helps. Change what does not.

Your perfect wind down routine is not the longest or prettiest one. It is the one you can repeat consistently enough that bedtime stops feeling like an abrupt switch.

This article is for informational purposes only and is not medical advice. If you regularly struggle to fall asleep, stay asleep, wake much earlier than intended, experience loud snoring or pauses in breathing during sleep, or feel excessively sleepy during the day despite allowing enough time for sleep, speak with a healthcare professional.
