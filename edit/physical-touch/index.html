Why Physical Touch Is More Important Than We Think

Physical affection is doing more for your mental health than you probably realize.

Think about how different you can feel after a real hug from someone you trust. You might still have the same problem, the same stressful day, and the same thoughts running through your head, but your body can feel a little calmer.

That response is not imaginary. Touch is one of the ways humans communicate comfort and connection, and researchers are finding that wanted physical touch can affect stress, anxiety, mood, and how supported we feel. The important word is wanted. Who is touching you, how you feel about them, and whether the contact feels safe all change the experience. [1]

A hug will not solve the problem in front of you. It can change how your body experiences the moment.

Your brain pays attention to touch

Your skin is constantly sending information to your brain, but social touch is not processed as a neutral sensation. The context matters. A hand on your shoulder from your best friend can feel reassuring. The same hand from someone you do not want touching you can feel uncomfortable immediately.

A 2023 review in Nature Reviews Psychology describes social touch as an important part of human development, communication, and psychological wellbeing. The researchers emphasize that its effect depends on the person giving the touch, the reason for it, and the situation it happens in. [1]

This is why the mental health conversation around touch should never be reduced to more touch is better. Safe, welcome touch is the part that matters.

Touch can affect your stress response

The strongest recent evidence comes from a 2024 review published in Nature Human Behaviour. Researchers combined 137 studies in a meta analysis involving 12,966 people and found that touch interventions had an overall beneficial effect on both physical and mental health. In adults, the largest benefits were seen for pain, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. [2]

The studies included different types of touch, and many involved massage or structured touch interventions, so this does not mean a hug should be treated like therapy. What it does show is that physical contact can have measurable effects on how we feel.

Research outside the lab points in the same direction. In a 2023 study that followed 104 college students in everyday life, days with more hugs were linked with a smaller cortisol awakening response the following morning. Cortisol is one of the hormones involved in your stress response. The study found an association, so it cannot prove the hugs caused the change, but it gives us a useful look at touch outside a controlled experiment. [3]

It can change how you feel in the moment too

Another 2023 study followed 247 adults throughout their normal day and repeatedly asked about affectionate touch and how they were feeling. In moments when participants reported more affectionate touch, they also reported less anxiety, stress, and general burden. They also showed higher salivary oxytocin. People who experienced more affectionate touch overall reported greater happiness and had lower cortisol levels. [4]

This was an observational study conducted during the COVID 19 pandemic, so it cannot tell us that touch alone caused those differences. Still, it supports something many people recognize in real life. Feeling physically connected to someone can change the emotional tone of a moment.

That can be a hug from your partner after work, holding your friend while she cries, sitting close to your mom on the couch, or reaching for someone's hand when you are nervous. Physical affection is not only romantic.

You do not need to turn affection into another wellness rule

This is probably the most useful part of the research.

You do not need a twenty second hug challenge. You do not need to track your oxytocin. You do not need to force yourself to cuddle when you want space.

If physical affection feels good to you, make sure it has a place in your life. Hug the people you love when you see them. Hold your partner's hand. Sit close. Put the phone down when you are cuddling. Let comforting touch actually be comforting instead of treating it like background noise.

The 2024 meta analysis also found that, across the studies it examined, having touch interventions more frequently was linked with greater benefits for some outcomes, while simply making each session longer was not consistently better. [2] That is a much more realistic message than trying to find a perfect number of seconds for a hug.

Physical touch is not a replacement for therapy, medication, sleep, friendship, or whatever else supports your mental health. It is one part of connection that is easy to overlook because it feels so ordinary.

So pay attention to how you feel after safe physical affection. If a hug leaves you calmer, if holding someone's hand makes a hard moment feel easier, or if you realize you have barely touched anyone you love lately, that information is useful.

You may not need another wellness habit. You might just need a little more connection.

This article is for informational purposes only and is not medical advice. Physical touch should always be wanted and consensual. Touch can support wellbeing, but it is not a substitute for professional mental health care.
